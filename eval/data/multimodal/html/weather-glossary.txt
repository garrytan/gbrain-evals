A short glossary of mountain weather terms

Forecasts for the high country use a handful of terms that mean something
narrower than their everyday sense. Knowing the exact definitions makes the
difference between reading a forecast and merely skimming it.
A lenticular cloud is a smooth lens shaped cap that forms over a summit
when moist air is forced up and over the peak. It often signals strong wind
aloft long before anything is felt in the valley. Graupel is soft rounded
snow pellet formed when supercooled droplets freeze onto falling flakes,
and it is a classic sign of the unstable air that builds spring storms.
The freezing level is the altitude where the air temperature crosses zero
degrees, and it matters more than the summit forecast on long climbs, since
rain below the freezing level becomes ice on every surface above it. A cold
front passage in the mountains commonly arrives with a wind shift first and
precipitation second, often less than an hour apart.

Lenticular caps over a ridge usually mean gusts above the saddle.
Graupel showers in the afternoon point to a thunderstorm cycle.
Plan turnaround times off the freezing level, not the valley high.
