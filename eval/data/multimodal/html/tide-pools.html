Life in the rocky intertidal zone

Tide pools form where a rocky shore traps seawater as the tide falls. The organisms that live in them survive some of the widest swings of any marine habitat, cycling twice a day between full submersion and exposure to sun, wind, and rain.

The upper pools are ruled by barnacles and periwinkle snails that can seal in moisture for hours. The middle zone belongs to mussels, hermit crabs, and the anemones that fold their tentacles inward at low tide to keep from drying out. Only the lowest pools, uncovered a few days each month, hold the slower giants such as the ochre sea star and the giant green anemone.

Visitors do the least harm by stepping only on bare rock, touching nothing, and never prying an animal from its hold. A sea star pulled from its rock can lose the tube feet it needs to hunt, and a crushed mussel bed takes years to close back over.

Field notes

  • Best viewing is in the hour before the lowest daylight tide.
  • Polarized sunglasses cut the surface glare and double what you can see.
  • Wet rock covered in algae is the most common cause of falls.
