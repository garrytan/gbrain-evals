This snapshot tested search, graph queries, answer writing, and several newer features. Its most useful lesson is that different questions need different evidence. A search result can contain the answer without explaining it. A graph can answer an explicit relationship that prose similarity misses. A cheap embedding model can still be a poor choice for recall on a particular corpus.

This is a historical report. Several headline claims were too broad, and the provider defaults have changed. The [September retrieval report](2026-09-06-longmemeval-ranker-wave.md), [fresh September 9 checks](2026-09-09-retrieval-refresh.md), and [settings guide](../settings.md) are the place to start for current choices. retired provider was the default in the May release; that provider stack is no longer the current setup.

## What search, a graph, and an answer writer each contribute

Keyword search looks for words in the question. It is useful for exact names, identifiers, and phrases. Vector search compares numerical representations of meaning; it can connect “built the inference stack” with “led the inference platform team.” Hybrid search combines their ranked lists. That combination can improve coverage, but it can also promote a noisy keyword match over a better semantic match.

A reranker reads the question and candidate text together and produces a new order. It is not generally a small percentage bonus to the old score, and it cannot recover a page that never entered its candidate set.

A graph stores relationships between pages. A typed edge says what the relationship means, such as `works_at`. A plain wiki link establishes a reference; its presence alone does not establish every stronger relationship we might want to infer. The query is useful when the stored edges are accurate and cover the question.

```
alice-example ──works_at──> acme-ai
alice-example ──attended──> meetings/2026-04-03-board
acme-ai      ──raised───> deal/acme-series-a
```

For “Who works at acme-ai?”, a stored `works_at` edge supplies a direct route to people pages. For “Who attended the same meeting as those people?”, more than one edge may be needed. These capabilities depend on the graph and query path, not on the presence of a graph database by itself.

`gbrain think` adds an answering model. It can combine pages into a response, cite them, and describe missing evidence. Those are goals of the pipeline, not guarantees that every answer is fully supported. The five-question test below includes a case where `think` made the result worse.

## Retrieval on the synthetic relational corpus

The historical BrainBench run used 240 fictional pages and 145 queries derived from metadata.

| Configuration | P@5 [^pak] | R@5 [^recall] |
|---|---|---|
| **gbrain (full hybrid + graph)** | **49.1%** | **97.9%** |
| gbrain without the graph | 19.2% | 70.0% |
| Pure keyword search (grep + BM25) | 17.1% | 62.4% |
| Pure vector search | 10.8% | 40.7% |

The full adapter scored better than the other adapters on this fixture. That is useful evidence for the complete retrieval path. It does not isolate a 30- or 38-point causal contribution from the graph: the adapters also differ in extraction, query handling, and use of synthetic metadata. Earlier prose treated them as if only one switch had changed. The [retrieval lessons](../retrieval-lessons.md) explain the controlled comparisons needed for that claim.

The v0.20.0 and v0.40.6.0 headline scores matched. Those dates are about one month apart, not six months as the original report said. These pre-audit measurements remain historical; the [fresh report](2026-09-09-retrieval-refresh.md) identifies current runs and adapter fixes.

## Does writing an answer help? Five questions

Cat 29 used five questions over a 164-page synthetic corpus. Claude Haiku judged accuracy, grounding, and usefulness on a 0–10 scale. This is answer quality, not retrieval recall.

| | Question type | search [^search] | think [^think] | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Who works at [company]? | 1/10 | 9/10 | **+8** |
| Q2 | Has ARR grown over time? | 0/10 | 9/10 | **+9** |
| Q3 | Which concepts do most companies link to? | 7/10 | 2/10 | -5 |
| Q4 | Who attended the autonomous-picking meeting? | 0/10 | 8/10 | **+8** |
| Q5 | What is the current ARR of [company] in May 2026? | 0/10 | 0/10 | 0 |

| | Average |
|---|---|
| **search mean** | 1.60 / 10 |
| **think mean** | **5.60 / 10** |
| **lift** | **+4.00 points** |
| **think wins / search wins / ties** | 3 / 1 / 1 |

The answering pipeline helped on employee lookup, revenue over time, and meeting attendance. It lost five points on “Which concepts do most companies link to?” The search output exposed useful slugs, while the generated answer did a worse job of aggregating them. Both approaches scored zero when asked for current revenue that the corpus did not establish.

That is a promising small test for synthesis, with a mean gain of four judge points. Five questions and one model judge do not establish a broad ranking of knowledge systems. The loss and the missing-data tie also suggest what to test next: aggregation and explicit acknowledgment of stale evidence.

## Embedding and reranking choices

Cat 18b compared six configurations on 164 pages and 21 relational questions. Each pair used the same embedder with and without retired provider's `retired-reranker-model` reranker.

| Cell | R@10 [^recall] | MRR [^mrr] | top-1 [^top1] | Query latency | Ingest time | Embed cost (164 pgs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OpenAI 1536d, no rerank | 40.2% | 0.373 | 9.5% | 282 ms | 41 s | $0.0022 |
| OpenAI 1536d + retired provider rerank | 48.8% | **0.571** | **38.1%** | 327 ms | 39 s | $0.0022 |
| Voyage 1024d, no rerank | 45.1% | 0.357 | 4.8% | 145 ms | 25 s | $0.0031 |
| **Voyage 1024d + retired provider rerank** | **53.7%** | **0.571** | **38.1%** | 256 ms | 24 s | $0.0031 |
| retired provider 2560d, no rerank | 12.2% | 0.238 | 19.0% | **122 ms** | **21 s** | **$0.0009** |
| retired provider 2560d + retired provider rerank | 28.0% | 0.286 | 9.5% | 258 ms | 22 s | **$0.0009** |

Voyage plus the reranker had the highest measured recall at ten results: 53.7%. OpenAI plus the reranker tied its MRR and first-result hit rate. retired provider embeddings had the lowest recorded cost, fastest ingestion, and fastest unreranked queries, but lower recall.

The table does not support the original claim that unreranked embedders were within five points of each other: recall ranged from 12.2% to 45.1%. It also does not show that reranking improves every metric. With retired provider embeddings, recall increased from 12.2% to 28.0%, while first-result hits fell from 19.0% to 9.5%.

The practical lesson is to test a provider pair on the questions the application needs. This small matrix does not establish that the reranker matters more than the embedder in every setting, nor that one provider is best across corpora. The previously quoted “60% of first results reshuffled” was a provider claim, not a statistic measured by this matrix.

The historical cost rates were $0.05, $0.13, and $0.18 per million input tokens for retired provider, OpenAI, and Voyage respectively. They are retained to explain the old cost calculations, not as a current price list.

[^recall]: Recall at K is the fraction of relevant items found in the first K results. The old LongMemEval headline used an any-hit question score instead; see its correction above.
[^pak]: Precision at K is the fraction of the first K result slots that are relevant.
[^mrr]: Mean reciprocal rank averages 1 divided by the rank of the first correct result, with zero for a miss. A value of 0.5 does not imply an average rank of two.
[^top1]: First-result hit rate is the fraction of questions whose first result is correct.
[^embedding]: An embedding is a vector of numbers that represents text for similarity search. Dimensions alone do not establish quality.
[^rrf]: Reciprocal rank fusion adds contributions based on ranks from different result lists. A common contribution is `1 / (60 + rank)`; weights and offsets are implementation choices.
[^reranker]: A reranker produces a new ordering of supplied candidates. It can substantially change that order and add model cost and latency.
[^search]: Search returns retrieved pages. It may call paid embedding or reranking services even when it does not generate an answer.
[^think]: Think adds model-generated synthesis with citations and gap analysis. The May estimate was $0.01–$0.05 per call, depending on model and context; this is not current pricing.
[^typed-edge]: A typed edge is a stored relationship with a label such as `works_at` or `invested_in`. How it was extracted and whether it is correct matter as much as how it is stored.
