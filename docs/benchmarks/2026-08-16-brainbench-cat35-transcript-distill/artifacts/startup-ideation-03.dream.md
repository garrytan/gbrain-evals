A pricing thesis surfaced during a debrief on [driftless](concepts/driftless-scheduling): the right unit to charge for is the unit of value delivery, not the unit of access. When those two units diverge, per-seat pricing creates perverse incentives that work against adoption. This mental model, surfaced by advisor [Mira Voss](people/mira-voss), has broad applicability beyond scheduling tools.

---

## The thesis

> "price per connected calendar, not per seat. She thinks seats create weird incentives where companies try to minimize who gets access, but calendars map directly to the value we provide."

**Per-seat pricing optimizes for the vendor's revenue.** Buyers respond by restricting access to minimize cost — which shrinks the product's footprint, reduces value delivery, and ironically makes churn more likely.

**Per-unit-of-value pricing aligns incentives.** Every calendar being managed by driftless is a calendar delivering value. The customer can't game the price by reducing calendars without also reducing the benefit they're paying for.

### Generalization

The principle: **identify what the product actually does for the customer at the atomic level, and charge for that thing**.

- For a scheduling-protection tool: the calendar
- For a freight-emissions tracker: the shipment or route
- For a document collaboration tool: maybe the document or the project, not the user

When the billed unit is the same as the delivered-value unit, the pricing model becomes self-evidencing. Usage growth = more value = higher bill, and the customer can see that equation clearly.

---

## The procurement threshold design

> "forty dollars per calendar per month for the pilot as a starting point. Her reasoning was that it is low enough that a team lead can expense it without procurement, but high enough to signal this is not some throwaway tool."

Pricing has two simultaneous jobs in an early pilot:

1. **Bypass friction** — stay below the expense threshold where procurement gets involved (~$500–$1000/month for a team is typically safe for a team lead to approve unilaterally)
2. **Signal seriousness** — a price that is too low reads as a toy, undermines trust, and attracts the wrong buyers

$40/calendar is a deliberate calibration of both. It's not aggressive enough to require a champion, but not cheap enough to be written off.

---

## The visibility corollary

> "visibility into the value is half the sale for tools like this."

When value is delivered to an atomic unit (each calendar), making that value visible closes the loop. A dashboard showing hours of scheduling overhead saved per calendar, per week — screenshottable, shareable — serves as:

- Ongoing confirmation for the buyer that the price is worth it
- Organic social proof when shared externally
- A metric for fundraising conversations (e.g., [Vantabrook](companies/vantabrook)'s scout program)

The pricing unit and the visibility surface should map to the same thing. If you charge per calendar, show per-calendar impact.
