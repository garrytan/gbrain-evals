A paid pilot is not just a revenue event — it is an internal ownership signal inside the buyer's organization. Free pilots collapse in procurement because no one at the buyer has budget skin in the game, which means no one is internally accountable for making the pilot succeed. This mental model came up in prep for [Anouk Verlinden](people/anouk-verlinden)'s negotiation with [Ostrafell](companies/ostrafell) on August 6, 2026.

## The Core Insight

> "My guidance is to anchor the pilot at ninety k, free pilots die in procurement because nobody owns them internally. We need them to have skin in the game from day one."

When a pilot is free, it lives in no one's budget. That means:
- No internal champion has staked their number on it succeeding
- Procurement has no existing precedent to point to for the full contract
- The deal can stall indefinitely because there's no urgency to resolve it

A paid pilot solves all three problems simultaneously. The budget owner who approved it is now invested in a positive outcome. Procurement already has a line item — conversion is incremental, not a new ask. And there's urgency because money is already on the table.

## Pricing Structure Implication

Fixed-fee pilot > per-unit pilot for closing speed. Per-unit structures invite the buyer to minimize the pilot scope to minimize cost — fixed fee commits them to the full scope upfront, generating richer data and stronger internal advocacy.

For the [Ostrafell](companies/ostrafell) deal: $90k fixed for 6 months across 40 stores (~$375/store/month). Simple, defensible, precedent-setting for expansion math later.

## The Procurement Psychology Loop

Paid pilot → named budget owner → named budget owner needs an exec sponsor to approve → exec sponsor is now internally accountable → exec sponsor becomes your internal champion for full rollout.

This is why identifying the exec sponsor early (see [reflection](wiki/personal/reflections/2026-08-06-coaching-anouk-ostrafell-pilot-6f5286)) is not just a process step — it's how the paid pilot's ownership logic actually closes.

## Related Principle: Low-Cost Deliverables as Value Anchors

> "I'd throw in a quarterly sustainability report, their brand team will love it. It costs us almost nothing to produce but gives them a deliverable."

The sustainability report is the inverse of the free pilot problem: a deliverable that costs the vendor almost nothing but gives the buyer's internal team something to show. It creates an additional stakeholder (the brand team) who has received value and will advocate for continuation — without touching the pricing anchor.

## Applicability

This model applies whenever:
- The buyer is a large org with formal procurement
- The deal requires internal budget approval (not a credit card swipe)
- There is meaningful internal politics around who "owns" vendor relationships

Free pilots are a trap that feels generous but undermines the deal's internal momentum from day one.

## Pricing Anchor

The anchor is $90k fixed fee for a six-month, forty-store pilot.

> "My guidance is to anchor the pilot at ninety k, free pilots die in procurement because nobody owns them internally. We need them to have skin in the game from day one."

Fixed-fee structure (not per-store) keeps it simple and avoids triggering expansion math before the pilot closes — though the per-store breakdown (~$375/store/month) is available as a defensibility response to ROI questions.

## Success Metrics

- **Efficiency gains** tied to route optimization and load consolidation
- **Sustainability KPI**: a 10% estimated emissions cut per store

> "The target is ten percent estimated emissions cut per store is the bar, based on route optimization and load consolidation. If we hit that, the story writes itself for their annual report."

**Value-add to include**: a quarterly sustainability report, essentially free to produce but gives [Ostrafell](companies/ostrafell)'s brand team a deliverable to wave around internally. Framed as an included bonus — sweetens the anchor without discounting.

> "I'd throw in a quarterly sustainability report, their brand team will love it. It costs us almost nothing to produce but gives them a deliverable."

## Procurement Navigation

Ostrafell's process is bureaucratic and will not move without a named exec sponsor.

> "their procurement will not move without a named exec sponsor so Anouk needs to surface that requirement early without sounding pushy."

Tactic: surface the sponsor organically with logistics framing — *"Who on your leadership team would we loop in for quarterly reviews?"*

Timeline expectation:

> "Anouk should expect a 4-6 week procurement cycle minimum after the pilot is verbally agreed. The key is to keep momentum by locking in next steps on every call."

If Ostrafell goes quiet, ping the exec sponsor directly — not the procurement contact.

## Identifying the Exec Sponsor

The fleet manager surfaced as the most likely exec sponsor based on her LinkedIn profile. Fleet ops is directly upstream of both efficiency metrics and emissions targets — she can champion the deal with operational credibility.

Watch for who asks the forward-looking questions ("what does expansion look like," "how do we measure this for the board") — that person is already thinking beyond the pilot.

> "the fleet manager smells like our exec sponsor based on her LinkedIn"

Likely sponsor archetypes at a retailer like [Ostrafell](companies/ostrafell): VP of Operations or head of sustainability initiatives.
