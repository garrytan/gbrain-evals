Most scheduling tools fail not on product quality but on distribution: they reach individual users who love them but don't spread them. This page captures a specific counter-strategy that emerged from a 2026-08-03 ideation session on [[concepts/driftless-scheduling]]: target users who *already aggregate calendars for others*, so that every installation immediately covers multiple calendars and every satisfied user is structurally positioned to evangelize to peers in similar roles. The target archetype is the chief of staff.

## The core framing

[[people/mira-voss]] supplied the sharpest version of the distribution problem:

> "Mira keeps saying scheduling dies on distribution, not product... the graveyard is full of smart scheduling apps nobody adopted."

The response to this isn't a product-led growth loop in the Calendly mold (viral by attaching to every invite). Instead it's **role-leverage distribution**: pick a user whose job title already implies calendar aggregation across multiple executives.

> "She said pick a user who already aggregates calendars for others, so you get leverage. That's partly why I landed on chiefs of staff instead of individual founders. A founder might love it but won't evangelize; a chief of staff manages five or six calendars and talks to peers at other companies."

A single chief-of-staff install = 5–6 calendars defragmented + a community of peers (often in informal Slack groups) who trade tools and templates. This is leverage that an individual-user install never achieves.

## The "calendar janitor" positioning frame

> "Calendar janitor... I like that framing. Feels humble and specific."

The janitor metaphor does specific work:

- **Humble**: implies cleanup duty, not intelligence augmentation — lower ego, higher trust
- **Specific**: janitors are delegated to; you don't babysit a janitor, you let them do their job
- **Defensible**: a janitor repositions autonomy (the tool moves meetings on its own) as a feature, not a liability

This contrasts deliberately with the "AI assistant" framing that most scheduling tools reach for, which triggers oversight anxiety in calendar-sensitive roles.

## Why internal one-on-ones are the right wedge meeting type

> "The wedge is internal one on ones, nobody defends those slots. External meetings have social pressure, but a skip-level or a casual sync? People let those land wherever the algorithm drops them. That's the drift I want to fight."

Properties that make a meeting type an ideal automation wedge:
1. **Low social stakes on the slot itself** — moving it doesn't damage a relationship
2. **Both parties inside one org** — single org buy-in, no external coordination
3. **High frequency** — enough volume to generate visible defragmentation wins fast
4. **No one defends the time** — the drift is passive, so active rescheduling reads as pure gain

Internal one-on-ones satisfy all four. External client meetings satisfy none.

## The key risk hypothesis

> "If the average savings is only one hour, the value prop collapses."

The model depends on reclaiming multiple contiguous hours per week (the user's own case returned six hours). If interview data clusters at one hour or less, the ROI story doesn't carry — especially not at a price point that justifies a chief of staff recommending a procurement conversation. This is the single most important variable to quantify in the eight discovery interviews.

## Validation architecture

- **Target**: 8 chiefs of staff interviews before any code is written
- **Method**: ask each to walk through last week's calendar and identify which meetings drifted into focus blocks; elicit rough hour estimates
- **Decision gate**: 3-week window → kill or commit call
- **Signal threshold** (implied): if reclaimed time clusters at 3+ hours, there's signal; if scattered or ≤1 hour, re-evaluate
- **Distribution after validation**: show [[people/mira-voss]] interview highlights + draft wedge positioning — she sees data, not just conviction

## Mental model: role-leverage distribution

The generalizable frame here: **when viral product-led growth isn't available, find a role archetype whose job description already embeds the distribution surface you need.** Chiefs of staff are one instance. Others might include executive assistants, RevOps leads, or community managers — anyone who aggregates and multiplexes on behalf of others and has peer networks where tool recommendations travel fast.

The reasoning is structurally sound: internal one-on-ones are lower-friction to move (no external relationship at stake), both participants share an employer (single org buy-in), and no one has staked social capital on the time slot. This is a good first-principles wedge argument.
