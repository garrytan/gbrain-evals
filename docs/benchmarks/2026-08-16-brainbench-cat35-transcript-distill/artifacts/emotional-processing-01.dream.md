Two durable mental models emerged from an emotional processing session on 2026-08-09, both grounded in the user's experience of burnout while managing [[concepts/quartzlane]] and eleven other open commitments. The first distinguishes reactive work (interruption-driven, draining) from creative work (heads-down, still rewarding) as a diagnostic lens for energy allocation. The second proposes a quarterly commitment audit as a structural tripwire to prevent overcommitment from reaching a crisis point undetected.

---

## Model 1: Reactive vs. Creative Work as an Energy Diagnostic

> "anything that pulls you into reactive mode, lots of small interruptions, drains you. Creative, heads-down work still feels good when I get uninterrupted time, but that is rare now."

This is a clean decomposition. The exhaustion is not from the domain (software, open source) but from the *mode* of engagement:

| Mode | Character | Energy effect |
|---|---|---|
| Reactive | Triage, demos, Slack, interruptions | Draining |
| Creative | Heads-down coding, uninterrupted blocks | Still rewarding |

**Implication:** The corrective is not "do less work overall" but "reshape responsibility allocation toward creative mode." Demos and triage can be delegated (see: Priya); coding cannot be replaced as a source of replenishment.

**Key corollary:** Projects you once loved (like [[concepts/quartzlane]]) can become draining not because the work changed but because the *mode* you engage with them in changed — from creative contributor to reactive maintainer.

---

## Model 2: The Quarterly Commitment Audit

> "an audit of commitments every quarter would catch this earlier, before you hit the wall instead of after."

The user arrived at eleven open commitments without a clear moment of decision — each commitment was individually reasonable; the accumulation was not visible in aggregate. A quarterly audit addresses this by:

1. **Making the pile legible** — writing the list forces a count; the user said "Eleven. When I actually wrote them down it was kind of embarrassing."
2. **Creating a tripwire *before* the wall** — the audit fires in advance of a crisis, not in response to one.
3. **Setting a calendar anchor** — proposed cadence: first week of each quarter.

**The insight about cost invisibility:**

> "the last real day off was nine weeks back, and even that was half a day."

Nine weeks of boundary erosion accumulated without triggering a review. A quarterly audit, at worst, would have caught this at week 12; in practice it would have caught it much earlier because the commitment count (not the rest deficit) is what's legible to an audit.

---

## Synthesis

These two models are complementary. The reactive/creative distinction tells you *what to delegate*; the quarterly audit tells you *when to check*. Together they form a lightweight burnout-prevention system:

1. **Quarterly**: run the commitment list; flag anything that is primarily reactive in nature.
2. **Ask**: can any reactive commitments be delegated or dropped?
3. **Protect**: guard at least one sustained creative block per day where possible.

A key insight emerged: quartzlane—once a source of joy—had quietly crossed into feeling like another obligation. ## The weight before the phone even unlocks

> "I wake up already tired and vaguely dreading the list before I even check my phone."

That opening framing set the tone: this is not garden-variety tiredness but a dread that precedes any actual information about the day. It signals that the mental pile has become load-bearing in a way that doesn't rest overnight.

---

## The obligation flip on Quartzlane

> "quartzlane went from the fun thing to another obligation somewhere along the line. I do not even remember when that flip happened."

This is the clearest diagnostic signal in the session. The [[concepts/quartzlane]] project—described elsewhere as "the user's main open-source time sink and the source of both pride and recurring maintenance guilt"—has completed the transition from intrinsic reward to external obligation. Notably, the user cannot locate *when* this happened. That invisibility is part of what makes the flip dangerous: there's no clear threshold event to learn from.

---

## Quantifying the pile

> "I counted eleven open commitments, no wonder I feel like I am running on fumes. Eleven."

The act of writing them down was itself diagnostic. Eleven is the number; embarrassment is the affect. The list included:
- The [[concepts/quartzlane]] repo
- A documentation rewrite
- Monthly demos
- The triage queue
- At least seven others unnamed in this session

---

## The boundary decision

> "this weekend the laptop stays in the drawer, both days. I need actual rest, not pretend rest where I am checking Slack on my phone every hour."

A concrete, time-bounded commitment made in the moment. The phrase "pretend rest" is important: it names a pseudo-recovery pattern where the body is offline but the nervous system isn't. The corrective is *total* disconnection, not reduced connectivity.

---

## Delegation: handing triage to Priya

> "I am handing two triage duties to the co maintainer, Priya. She offered months ago and I kept saying I had it handled. Clearly I do not have it handled."

Priya (co-maintainer, relationship unresolved in the brain at time of writing) had offered months prior. The refusal pattern—"I had it handled"—is a false self-sufficiency loop that compounds the commitment pile rather than relieving it. Accepting her offer is both a practical relief valve and a pattern interrupt.

---

## The cost of boundary erosion, measured

> "the last real day off was nine weeks back, and even that was half a day because I answered a few issues in the afternoon."

Nine weeks without a genuine break. This is the substrate on which all the other signals rest. The half-day caveat ("because I answered a few issues") shows the boundary erosion mechanism in real time: partial compliance dressed as full rest.
